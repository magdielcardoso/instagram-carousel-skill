# Audience, value, and proof

Read this reference when a carousel is meant to persuade, generate consideration, promote an event, or move someone toward a business action. It guides message strategy and sequence; use `design-distillation.md` for the visual system. For educational or informational carousels, use only the audience-fit and evidence principles. Do not force a sales funnel, urgency, or revenue framing into an explainer.

## Plan the message before styling it

- Start with a real audience need: the person's job, trigger, problem, attempted solution, or desired outcome. A specific buying or learning situation is usually more useful than a broad demographic label. Do not invent customer research when the brief does not provide it.
- Estimate what the audience already knows: the problem, possible solution, product, or next step. Lead with language and context they can recognize, then supply the information or proof that helps them move one step forward. Do not lead with internal product terms when the audience does not yet know why they matter.
- Decide the desired progress for this carousel before choosing its headline, visual reference, or CTA. The sequence should serve both the audience's need and the brand's legitimate goal.

## Make the promise concrete and support it

- Prefer a specific problem, outcome, use case, or mechanism over generic claims such as “complete platform,” “revolutionary,” or “for everyone.” Translate a feature into what the person can do or understand with it.
- Keep every promise within the available proof. Demonstrate the mechanism, use a real product view, cite a verifiable outcome, or narrow the claim. If the evidence is missing, use a modest factual statement instead of inventing a metric or testimonial.
- Match the kind of proof to the claim: an interface capture can show a workflow; a process diagram can explain a mechanism; a case or metric can support an outcome only when its source, context, and permission are known. A decorative mockup is not evidence of a real capability.
- Make differentiation concrete. Show the meaningful difference in mechanism, experience, or trade-off rather than saying the offer is simply better. Use comparisons only when they are fair and supported.

## Sequence value before the ask

- Give the audience useful context, explanation, or proof before asking for a commercial action. A flexible arc is recognizable need → useful answer or mechanism → evidence/context → next step. Skip stages that the facts do not support.
- Keep one main claim per slide and attach its proof or explanation nearby. Let the order answer likely questions instead of piling unrelated benefits into one screen.
- Write the CTA as the next action, not a vague label. Say what to do and, when relevant, what happens next or what it requires. State terms such as price, duration, or “no card” only when confirmed.
- For save, comment, share, or follow requests, give a clear reason tied to the content. Do not promise engagement outcomes or add a second CTA that competes with the primary one.
- Use real urgency and social proof only when verified and current. Never manufacture scarcity, deadlines, endorsements, ratings, or user counts to shorten deliberation.

## Keep the framework within its purpose

This guidance is an adaptation for carousel messaging, not a license to optimize every post for conversion. For a tutorial, checklist, or explainer, clarity and useful learning are the goal; a CTA may simply invite the reader to save or discuss the material.

Do not apply this source-derived revenue/conversion framework to gambling, betting, casinos, or real-money games of chance, including loot-box or real-money-gaming mechanics. This boundary follows the source author's stated reuse condition. Other independent guidance in this carousel skill remains separate from this framework.
